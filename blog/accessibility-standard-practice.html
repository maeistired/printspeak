Making Accessibility Standard Practice: Why Aren't Talking Labels on Every Product?

November 3, 2026 · 5 min read

By Richard Gao

Try identifying a food product, a medication, or a household item without reading the label on it. Was it difficult to distinguish them? This is a regular problem for many people with visual impairment. Although there are already existing technologies like speaking labels and sound labeling systems to counteract this issue, their use is still not common in consumer products. As companies are increasingly moving towards inclusive and user focused design practices, the question stands: why are there no speaking labels?

Barriers Preventing Widespread Adoption

Despite the advantages, there are some challenges that prevent talking labels from becoming a norm in the packaging industry.

1. Production and Implementation Costs

The cost is one of the most frequently mentioned challenges when referring to talking labels. Many companies consider making their packaging accessible to be an additional expense on top of their current production costs. This is particularly true when companies have to come up with new packaging designs and use new technologies.

Manufacturers who have small budgets may consider accessibility improvements as unimportant and unnecessary (Young et al., 2023). Because of this, accessibility considerations tend to be introduced as part of a new initiative or through a limited number of products.

2. Lack of Awareness and Understanding

Most packaging manufacturers or designers do not know the everyday problems that individuals with visual impairments go through (Roland DG, 2024). Studies and advocacy groups constantly observe that packaging is designed primarily for those who can see, but leave critical information inaccessible to others (Plečko et al., 2023).

Businesses risk underestimating the demand for accessible packaging, consumer independence, and safety without direct involvement from the targeted consumers themselves (Plečko et al., 2023).

3. Technical Limitations

With aspects such as health and safety, marketing messages, and branding being investigated for an accessible packaging approach, particular attention must be paid to the equipment and activities that support and enforce this change.

Implementing these technologies, such as accessible talking labels or RFID tags, will continue to pose various challenges. Realistically, items such as talking labels may be technologically alien to some consumers, preventing ease of use when combined with existing phones and new apps. Further issues such as ensuring compatibility over different devices and operating systems present an additional source of complexity for manufacturers.

In certain situations, consumers will have to familiarize themselves with new technologies in order to gain the benefits of various alternatives, leading to additional problems in the acceptance of such technology.

4. Accessibility Is Often Treated as an Add-On

One of the main issues is that the majority of companies consider accessibility at the end of the packaging design process. If companies regard accessibility as a matter of choice, it gives them the opportunity to focus on other business needs or marketing goals.

What Can Designers, Manufacturers, and Businesses Do?

1. Design with Accessibility from the Start

These designs should be considered during the early stages rather than at the end of the design process. Incorporating NFC and/or RFID technologies in their packaging enables them to deliver product information in audio. The use of talking labels, along with large and contrasting letters, tactile symbols and Braille, can make packages more accessible for people with visual impairments (Plečko et al., 2023).

2. Collaborate with Accessibility Organisations

Businesses should collaborate with organisations like the Royal National Institute of Blind People to get to know the needs of people with visual impairments. Collaboration with these organizations can help businesses conduct tests of talking tags to find out how to improve the audio clarity and convenience of using the products. Consulting the persons with different access needs can help designers find out how to make their talking labels more usable for different types of packages from food to medicine (NaviLens, n.d.).

3. Recognise Accessibility as a Business Opportunity

Talking labels are not only helpful for the blind and visually impaired, but also helps elderly people, people with reading difficulties, and people who prefer to hear information. The printed text on the package is supplemented with audio information, which means that it will provide the customer with a better experience and make it easier for them to identify and understand the product. The use of NFC technology can help companies prove that adding in inclusive design, can increase customer satisfaction, and attract more customers.

Summary

As technology continues to reinvent itself, the use and knowledge of talking labels have not been circulating in packaging considerations. Why is that so? And what can businesses, manufacturers, and designers do to implement this in their packaging?
